Restarting and resuming jobs¶

A failed job is not lost. It stays in the workspace with all its files, and an operator can fix what went wrong and revive it with a request. This page shows where a failed job is, what you may edit, which request to use, and how to resume a parent whose child failed. The request mechanics are in Task-manager usage in detail; the protocol is in Workflow filesystem API in detail.

Where a failed job is and how to read it¶

A failed job stays in jobs/failed/<placement>/...; workspace gc does not remove it. Three commands tell you what happened:

httk job show silicon        # prints the job directory
httk job why silicon         # explains the failure
httk job log silicon         # prints the run log

For a parent that failed at its wait, job show also lists its kept join and children.

The job directory holds:

Path

Content

job.json

The immutable declaration: workflow, parameters, placement, retry policy.

state.json

The owner-written state: current step, failure, counters, join.

run/

The persistent workdir, a.workdir in the runner.

attempts/<A>/

The control area of attempt A, including error.json.

logs/

The run log.

data/

The results the job committed.

.httk-job/

The runner’s own state, a.state, and the seal.

workflow/

Only in an unlocked job: its own copy of the workflow’s runner (see Debug mode).

What you may edit¶

Everything in the job directory may be edited while the job is failed or paused, except job.json. It is digest-pinned at the first activation, and a different digest fails the job with protocol_error. That includes the retry policy, the parameters and the placement. The exception is an unlocked job (see Debug mode), whose job.json may be edited.

Edit before posting the request: a manager may launch the job the moment it is revived.

A revived step reruns from its top. Whatever the step itself regenerates, such as copying a template or writing inputs from parameters, it regenerates again and overwrites a hand edit made at that layer. Edit at the layer the step reads: a payload file under files/ that a step copies from, rather than the copy in run/; a file in run/ (an INCAR, say) only when the step uses it as it is. The runner’s documentation says which files a step regenerates.

The retry budgets in job.json (maximum_attempts_per_activation, maximum_total_attempts, maximum_activations; absent means unbounded) are not used up by a revival: each revival gives the job its declared allowance again from that point, unless you ask it not to with --keep-budget (see Retry budgets on revival).

The three verbs¶

httk job request continue --reason "fixed the input" silicon
httk job request restart --reason "redo this step" silicon
httk job request override_step --override-step relax --reason "redo relax" silicon

Each applies to a failed or paused job. The CLI applies it at once to a job no manager holds and prints applied, queued, refused or uncertain; a job its owner holds applies it at the next boundary.

Action

Effect

continue

A job that failed at its wait on children (see below) returns to waiting with its retained join: no new attempt. Any other failed job, and a paused job, returns to ready and the current step reruns as a new attempt.

restart

Always ready: the current step reruns as a new attempt. The failure is cleared and a retained join is dropped.

override_step --override-step STEP

ready with a new activation of STEP. STEP is checked against the steps the runner recorded unless --force. A retained join is dropped.

For a job that did not fail at a wait, continue and restart are the same. The attempt reason is manual_continue for both. Each of the three first restores the job’s declared retry allowance, so the revived job may again run as many attempts and activations as it was declared with; --keep-budget revives it within the limits it has. job why says what the limits are after the revival.

--force applies to all three. It accepts the revival hazard described next, and publishes an override_step outside the recorded steps. Cancelled jobs cannot be revived by any request.

Retry budgets on revival¶

A revival counts what the job has already started and raises each declared limit so the declared number of units is available again from here. With maximum_attempts_per_activation = 3, a job that failed with retry_exhausted after attempt 3 runs attempts 4, 5 and 6 after a restart; one that failed ordinarily at attempt 1 runs attempts 2, 3 and 4. In detail, for each limit with a declared value D, the new effective limit is the larger of the current one and U + D, where U counts the units already started in the scope the next unit lands in:

Limit

U

maximum_attempts_per_activation

The attempts started in the current activation; 0 for override_step and for a continue that reopens a wait, whose next attempt opens a new activation.

maximum_total_attempts

The attempts started in total.

maximum_activations

The activations that have a started attempt.

An attempt that was recorded but never started (a retry that settled just before a pause, or a launch refused by a lowered budget) is the next unit, not a used one, for continue and restart, which run it. override_step abandons it, and the counters keep its slot, so for override_step U is the attempts and activations recorded so far, started or not. So a continue that reopens a wait (after dependency_failure or budget_exhausted) raises maximum_activations to the activations so far plus D, giving the join D more activations, and override_step gives the declared attempts in the new activation and D activations counting the one it opens. job why on a parent that failed at its wait lists the reopenable wait and, as operator restart, the limits a restart would leave.

The raise is written to state.json budget_override like a set_budget (see below), and the request_applied history entry carries the limits it changed as budget. A limit already higher, or lifted to unlimited, is left alone; a limit job.json declares unlimited is restored to unlimited, which drops an override that capped it. Automatic retries by the manager never raise a limit.

--keep-budget (on continue, restart and override_step, and on continue --tree, where it applies to every descendant) leaves the limits as they are. The revival is then bound by them: a rerun past a limit fails right back with retry_exhausted or budget_exhausted, and a wait that failed with budget_exhausted re-decides under the same maximum_activations.

Raising a budget¶

A revival restores the declared allowance by itself. To give a job more than its declaration allows, or a different budget while it is ready or running, raise the limit with a request instead, since job.json cannot be edited:

httk job why silicon
httk job request set_budget --max-attempts-per-activation 3 --reason "flaky node" silicon

  1. job why names the budgets against their effective limits.

  2. set_budget raises the limit. The job stays where it is, in the state and at the priority it had, and no attempt starts.

  3. A later revival does not lower it: the raise to the declared allowance only ever raises.

set_budget takes at least one of --max-attempts-per-activation N, --max-total-attempts N and --max-activations N. Each is an integer of 1 or more, or unlimited to lift that limit. The options are valid only with set_budget. The request applies to any job that is not succeeded or cancelled, including a ready or running one; on a running attempt it applies at the next boundary, like every request. It does not need --force.

The values go into state.json as budget_override. Later requests merge into it: a key set again takes the later value, and a key not named keeps its earlier value. A limit with no override falls back to job.json. The effective policy is the declared limits with the override applied. The manager’s launch check, automatic retries, the maximum_activations checks and the revival checks of continue, restart and override_step all use it. job.json and the provenance record of the job keep the declared policy.

job why and job show print the budget counters against the effective limit, and the declared one where it differs:

attempts this activation  1/3 (declared 1)

The JSON output carries the declared values and budget_override in its budgets mapping. Each applied set_budget, and each revival that raised a limit, records the limits it set in the job’s state.json history (the request_applied entry carries budget).

A parent that failed at its wait keeps its join, and continue reopens it with no new attempt. A parent that failed with budget_exhausted (its join would advance past maximum_activations) is reopened the same way: the continue restores the declared allowance, so the join re-decides with room for its next activation and advances to its next step, with no new children:

httk job request continue --reason "one more step" PARENT

With --keep-budget, raise maximum_activations with set_budget first. restart reruns the spawning step, which reuses its existing children (see Restarting the parent instead).

A limit can also be lowered. A limit set below what the job has already used ends the job at its next budget check: a ready job fails with budget_exhausted at its next claim. That is a way to stop further automatic retries. It holds only until the next revival without --keep-budget, which restores the declared allowance.

Do not try the other routes. Editing job.json fails the job at the digest check, unless the job is unlocked (see Debug mode). Counters are not reset, since that would lose the record of what the job did; a revival raises the limits instead. --force does not skip a budget: it only accepts the revival hazard, and the manager checks the budget again at launch.

Switching the workflow¶

A job runs the installed workflow its job.json names, and job.json cannot be edited. To make existing jobs run another installation, such as a newer version of the same workflow, post set_workflow. To bump every ready job to the newest installation of a release:

httk workflow install git+https://github.com/ORG/REPO@v1.3.0#relax
httk job request set_workflow --workflow relax@v1.3.0 --state ready --reason "bump to v1.3.0"

To repair failed jobs with a fixed version, switch them, then restart them:

httk job request set_workflow --workflow relax@v1.3.1 --state failed --reason "fixed runner"
httk job request restart --state failed --reason "rerun on v1.3.1"

--workflow takes an installed id, a short name (the newest installation of that name) or NAME@REF. It is resolved once, in the job’s workspace, and the request names the installation’s id. Jobs are selected by JOB_ID arguments, by --state STATE (repeatable), by --step STEP (repeatable) and by --placement PREFIX, as for job transfer: JOB_ID and --state are joined, --step narrows both and alone selects from every unowned state, and --placement narrows --state or a --step given without JOB_ID. Every action of job request takes them, so --state ready --step relax bumps only the ready jobs that will run relax.

Every selected job is checked before any request is posted, and the job’s owner checks again when it applies the request. The candidate must:

  1. be installed in the job’s workspace;

  2. carry the same declaration $id as the job’s stored declaration, or, when the job’s declaration has no $id, have the same short name;

  3. declare the same inputs as the job’s declared.inputs: the same names, payload destinations and hook-consumed inputs;

  4. declare the same output names as the job’s stored declaration;

  5. declare no parameter that the job has no value for, unless the job’s own declaration also had no value for it, and keep every parameter the job’s declaration lists, each value matching the declared type;

  6. declare the same environment names and types;

  7. declare the step the job runs next; for a parent waiting on its children (or failed at its wait), the steps its join advances to.

A job made from an installed workflow stores its declaration and records empty parameter, input and environment declarations by leaving them out. A job that stores no declaration, such as a spawned child, is compared with its current installation instead; when that is gone too, the switch is refused as unverifiable. A refusal names the rule that failed. Switching a job back to the workflow its job.json names removes the override. A job that runs a language realization (CWL, PWD, jobflow, httk v1) is never switched, since its executed document lives in the payload.

The request applies to any job that is not succeeded or cancelled. It does not need --force. An unowned job is switched at once; a job a manager holds is switched at its next boundary, and a running attempt finishes on the code it started with. A waiting parent can be switched; its children are separate jobs that keep their own workflows. Children that the job spawns after the switch run the new installation (a restarted step reuses its existing children, which keep their workflow; delete them to get children of the new one), and a.call uses the new installation’s [workflow.calls].

What changes is the runner code and its calls. What stays as created is the payload, the parameters, the declarations, resources and step_resources (a larger maxtime in the new version is not used; job show shows the job’s resources). An instantiate hook of the new version is not run again; the command warns when the candidate has one. Implementations that share one declaration $id can differ in language, so a switch can move a job, for example, from a Python runner to a Bash one.

The choice is written to state.json as workflow_override {id, name}, and the recorded runner_steps of the old runner are cleared. job show prints the declared workflow and the one the job runs. Collection uses the installation that produced the outputs, and the collected job record keeps the declared workflow with workflow_override beside it. A job that carries an override cannot be ejected into a workspace daemon’s exchange, whose adoption rebuilds state.json; the workspace daemon protocol does not carry set_workflow.

Debug mode: unlocked jobs¶

The rules that keep a job’s provenance intact (a digest-pinned job.json, a succeeded job that is never changed, the set_workflow compatibility rules, a pinned workflow tree) get in the way while you develop or debug a workflow. Unlocking a job lifts them for that job and marks it, so that collection does not store it by accident:

httk job unlock --reason "debugging relax" silicon   # marker and a runner copy
httk job unlock --no-copy silicon                   # marker only
httk job request unlock --reason "debugging relax" silicon   # the same, ancestors included

job unlock posts an unlock request per selected job and applies it at once to a job no manager holds; it prints and exits as job detach does, and takes --reason TEXT (default job unlock). job request unlock posts the same request, and on a local workspace unlocks the ancestors too, with the same lines and exit status. A remote workspace is refused by job unlock as by job detach; job request unlock reaches an ssh remote through the signed request envelope, but is refused there for a spawned job (unlock spawned jobs on the workspace host with httk job unlock, which also unlocks their ancestors). A workspace daemon does not carry unlock (run it on the daemon host). A cancelled job cannot be unlocked, and a succeeded job with a live seal must be released with job unseal first. A second unlock of a job that already has its copy, or was unlocked with --no-copy, is dropped as already unlocked; an unlock with the copy missing makes a fresh copy.

The marker is one-way. state.json records unlocked with the time, the operator and the reason. No request clears it, and it survives the repair of a damaged state.json. job show prints an unlocked row (since and by, or inherited from, whether it runs its own runner copy, and that collection needs --force), job why has a “debug mode” check, and the JSON report carries unlocked and paths.runner_copy (<payload>/workflow). To get an ordinary job again, make a new one.

Ancestors and children. job unlock also posts a marker-only request to every ancestor of each selected job, nearest first, following the parent recorded in job.json (also for a detached job). Ancestor lines end with (ancestor); an ancestor already unlocked is skipped (already unlocked (ancestor)), and a parent that cannot be found is reported without changing the exit status. Each refusal is printed per job, and the command exits 1 if any request was refused. Children that an unlocked parent publishes later start out unlocked too (unlocked.inherited_from names the parent); they get no runner copy.

Jobs with an embedded workflow. A job created with job new --embed-workflow (see Workflow packages in detail) already has its workflow in workflow/, so job unlock keeps that tree (existing runner copy kept; --no-copy is the same) and never looks anything up in the store; with the tree missing, a copying unlock is dropped (the embedded workflow/ is missing; nothing to copy). Edit workflow/ and restart. From then on the job launches the tree unverified and the manager re-pins its digest with source "copy", like any unlocked copy. set_workflow is refused for an embedded-workflow job, with or without the unlock: change workflow/ instead.

What it relaxes¶

  • job.json may be edited at any time. The next reconcile pins the new digest and records job_digest_repinned ({from, to}) in the history. A job.json that cannot be read still fails the job.

  • continue, restart and override_step, and also set_budget and set_workflow, apply to a succeeded job. The revival hazard described above still needs --force.

  • set_workflow skips every compatibility check beyond the installation of the candidate: the ones listed above and the check of the call slots. The candidate must still be installed, and a job that runs a language realization is still refused.

  • A succeeded unlocked job is not sealed (seal is {"disabled": true}), and job seal is refused. job delete of it needs no job unseal.

Editing the runner¶

The default unlock copies the job’s installed package/ to workflow/ at the top of the job directory, next to job.json, run/ and data/, so that a listing shows that the job carries its own code. workflow is a reserved payload name: inputs, staged_files and --input-move-from-job paths cannot target it, and a v1 template package with a top-level workflow member is refused at job preparation. An existing copy is never overwritten (a first unlock that finds one keeps it and reports job unlocked; existing runner copy kept, so you may place your own runner tree there before unlocking), and a language realization (CWL, PWD, jobflow, httk v1) gets none. From then on, while the job is unlocked, the manager launches the runner from that copy (execution from workflow/ is not automatic: only an unlocked job that has the directory runs from it, and a workflow/ in a locked job is inert), and workflow_pin records the digest of the copy at launch (with source "copy", against "installed" for the installation), without the installation’s declared build artifacts. Only the runner itself comes from the copy: the build spec, platform probe and build artifacts of a compiled workflow still come from the installation in the store, so editing [workflow.build] in the copy has no effect; rebuild through the installation. Edit the copy in place and revive the job:

httk job unlock silicon
$EDITOR "$(httk job show silicon)/workflow/run.py"
httk job request restart --reason "try the fix" silicon

In a confined attempt and its ranks workflow/ is a read-only overlay, like job.json and logs/: job-local code runs only inside the job’s own sandbox, and nothing a running attempt writes there is trusted afterwards. Place or edit the copy between attempts; you may also place your own tree before unlocking. An attempt that leaves a workflow/ that did not exist at its launch (workflow_pin workflow_present false) fails with protocol_error (“the attempt created workflow/; it was removed”), its outcome is voided as for an attempt that rewrites job.json, and the created workflow/ is deleted. The same check runs when the manager recovers a crashed running attempt, and when it rebuilds a damaged state.json it also deletes workflow/ unless the salvaged pin says it was present at launch. A tree present at launch is never touched; it was read-only for the attempt. A spawned child staged with a top-level workflow/ is refused.

The installed workflow is untouched, so other jobs of it are not affected, and set_workflow is refused while the copy exists, by the CLI before anything is posted: edit or remove the copy instead. The copy is not fsynced, so after a power loss it may be torn: remove workflow/ and run job unlock again. --no-copy is for debugging with data and job.json only.

What stays yours¶

Nothing clears data/, run/ or the children a job already published when a step reruns; delete what must not survive. Still enforced: that only the owner writes state.json, that requests apply once, the refusal of symlinks and other unsafe entries, the placement and schema rules, the sandbox overlays, cancelled as a final state, the retry budgets and the digests of staged inputs. job new --input-move-from-job out of an unlocked job is refused (copy the file instead), and so is ejecting it into a workspace daemon’s exchange.

Collecting an unlocked job¶

httk collect stores nothing for an unlocked job. It reports the job as degraded, job is unlocked (debug mode); pass --force to collect it, and exits nonzero; collection with --postprocess reports the same text as an error, and --force lets both collect the job. httk collect --force collects it through its workflow’s registered collector as usual; the collect hook of the runner copy is used only with --allow-job-collector, unverified, only when no registered provider supplies a collector, and only after the job has run again from the copy (workflow_pin source "copy") with the copy unchanged since that launch. Unlocking a finished job and collecting straight away degrades the job with the job's last attempt did not run from its runner copy; restart it before collecting. Other reasons read runner copy modified since the job ran: ... digest X does not match pinned Y and ... could not be verified. An unlocked job without a copy uses the installed tree as before. With --into --force the run is stored without any mark that the job was unlocked: you take responsibility for it. Every per-job line of an unlocked job carries "unlocked": true, forced or not. --raw and campaign collection are readouts and do not check the marker. See Collecting results.

Parents and children¶

A parent that waits on children with gather() fails when its join can no longer be satisfied, because a child failed or was cancelled. The parent records what it saw of each child (observations) and, when the gather named no on_impossible step, fails with dependency_failure at its wait. It keeps its join in state.json, and that retained join is what lets you reopen the wait. The children stay where they failed. A child that runs while its parent is failed still finds it through a.parent, since the parent stays at its placement. A parent can also fail at its wait with budget_exhausted: its join is satisfied (or rescued), but the advance would pass maximum_activations. It keeps its join then too; see Raising a budget.

Fix the child, then continue the parent¶

httk job why PARENT
httk job request continue --reason "fixed the INCAR" CHILD
httk job show CHILD
httk job log CHILD
httk job request continue --reason "child succeeded" PARENT

  1. job why PARENT says the job failed while waiting on its children, lists each child that did not succeed with its failure, and notes that continue reopens the wait while restart reruns the step, which reuses the children that still exist.

  2. Fix the child as described above, then continue it. Reviving a child that the parent’s recorded observations name needs --force, unless that parent’s join still names the child: the parent failed at its wait, or waits on it again. This flow needs no --force. Continuing the parent first is accepted too, but the manager re-decides the reopened wait within one pass and, while the child is still failed, fails the parent again at once. That is harmless: continue it again after the child succeeded.

  3. Watch the child with job show and job log until it succeeds.

  4. continue the parent. It returns to waiting with the same join, with no new attempt. On its next pass the manager re-evaluates the join from the children’s current states. If it is satisfied, the gathering step runs with the children as a.children, as usual. If a child is still failed or cancelled, the parent fails again with dependency_failure at once.

Without --tree, continue on such a parent prints a note on stderr for each join child that is still failed or cancelled, or not found at its placement, so you know the reopened wait will fail again.

Nested trees¶

Failures propagate upward: when a child’s own wait fails, that child fails with dependency_failure, and its parent’s join becomes impossible in turn. Every level keeps its join. To resume the whole chain at once:

httk job request continue --tree --reason "inputs fixed" PARENT

--tree also resumes the failed descendants reachable through retained joins, deepest first, before the parent. Each is an ordinary continue request, posted and served one job at a time in that order. If a descendant’s request is not applied (queued behind its owner, refused, or its revival fails at a budget), the CLI stops, posts nothing for the remaining jobs, prints not posted: <job_key> ... for each and exits 1; resolve that job and rerun continue --tree. A budget refusal happens only with --keep-budget, which --tree forwards to every continue it posts. Children in other states (succeeded, paused, running, cancelled) are left alone. Join children --tree leaves alone (not found, cancelled, or held by an owner at that moment) are reported with a note, as without --tree. Fix the leaf failures first: a descendant that fails for its own reason, not at a wait, returns to ready and reruns its step, which may fail again. --tree is valid only with continue.

Restarting the parent instead¶

restart on a parent that failed at its wait reruns the spawning step. A child label is unique per parent job, and a spawn or call whose label names an existing child reuses it: nothing new is created and the new specification is ignored. A failed or cancelled child would be reused too, so the parent would fail at its wait again. restart therefore refuses, and posts nothing, when the parent recorded such a child: it prints one error: line per failed or cancelled child (its label, job key, state and failure code) and the remedy, then exits non-zero (1; 2 over ssh, where the far side’s lines are relayed):

$ httk job request restart --reason "redo" PARENT
error: x x--<uuid> is failed dependency_failure; a restart reruns the spawning step, which reuses it
resume failed descendants and the parent with 'httk job request continue --tree --reason TEXT PARENT'; ...

Revive the child first (continue it, or continue --tree the parent), or delete it with httk job delete so the restarted step creates a fresh one. --force restarts anyway; the runtime then logs a warning for each failed or cancelled child it reuses. The check also applies to jobs selected with --state or --step, and to a remote workspace over ssh. A child that no longer exists is created anew. The restart restores the declared allowance, so it runs even with maximum_attempts_per_activation = 1; with --keep-budget the spawning step’s attempt counts against the current limit, and such a restart fails right back with retry_exhausted. Alternatively, httk job request override_step --override-step <spawning step> reruns that step as a new activation. The restarted step waits on the reused children again. Between the restart and the publication of the new wait, reviving one of them still needs --force; revive the child before restarting the parent and no --force is needed.

To get several children that share a name, number the labels. A restart of this step reuses site-0, site-1, … that still exist:

for index, site in enumerate(sites):
    a.spawn(ChildSpec(step="relax", parameters={"site": site}), label=f"site-{index}")
a.gather("collect")

Cancelled and deleted children¶

A cancelled child cannot be revived by any request, so a join naming it stays impossible: continue on the parent fails again at once. A restart or override_step of the spawning step reuses the cancelled child too (restart refuses unless --force): delete it first, then restart the parent or override_step it. A deleted child makes a reopened wait fail with dependency_failure after the join grace, a manager option that defaults to one hour.

Parents with on_impossible¶

A parent whose gather named an on_impossible step advanced to that step instead of failing, which consumed the observation and cleared the join (unless that advance failed with budget_exhausted, which keeps it). Reviving its child then still needs --force, and the parent does not notice the revived child. The wait cannot be reopened. To have the parent use the revived child, rerun a step that gathers it again (a runner step that calls gather(..., rejoin=[label]), via override_step), or delete the child and restart or override_step the spawning step to create a new one (without the delete the step reuses it).

Paused jobs¶

A job pauses on a pause request or when the runner calls a.pause(reason). It stays where it is until you continue it, which reruns the current step as a new attempt, or restart it, which does the same; edit its files first, as for a failed job. override_step moves it to a named step.

httk job request continue --reason "quota restored" silicon

Remote workspaces¶

continue --tree works on a remote workspace reached over ssh as it does locally: the far side expands the tree, your machine signs only continue requests (the selected jobs and their failed descendants, each noted on stderr), and the far side serves them one at a time, deepest first, stopping at the first that does not resume. A workspace daemon remote refuses --tree; there, post one continue per failed job yourself, deepest first, then the parent, or run it on the daemon host. The restart refusal above runs on the far side of an ssh remote too. --state, --step and --placement also select jobs of local workspaces only; give job ids for a remote one. set_workflow resolves --workflow on the remote side, against the remote workspace’s installations; it is refused for a workspace daemon remote. Remotes covers what a remote binding accepts. A job lost on a remote after job push is returned from its away copy with httk job recover JOB and pushed again.
